KOROR, Palau — Palau’s hospital has resolved its recent medicine shortage, with 98% of required medicines now in stock, but the Ministry of Health and Human Services cannot guarantee a consistent supply as the government’s current budget restrictions delay the next orders.

Sonsorol Governor with women from Sonsorol at the MHHS Townhall in Echang, Koror. Oct. 29, 2026.

Claret Matlab, director of Hospital Services at the Ministry of Health and Human Services, said the hospital received its medicine supplies shortly after reporting that its stocks had dropped to 46%.

The shortage was driven by supply problems in the United States, including limited availability of medicines and the prioritization of domestic supplies, Matlab said.

Dir. Claret Matlab

The latest stock level marks a significant recovery from the shortage.

However, the recovery does not necessarily mean Palau is protected from another shortage. Matlab did not offer assurances that medicine supplies would remain consistent or that the hospital would avoid similar problems in the future.

With 98% of required medicines now in stock, the immediate shortage has been resolved, according to Matlab. The next challenge is ensuring that the hospital can replenish supplies without delays caused by funding restrictions or external supply problems.

Sonsorol Governor with constituent during the Ministry of Health Townhall on Oct. 29.

Key questions include when the government will release the funding needed for pending orders, how much medicine remains on order and whether the hospital has contingency measures for future supply disruptions.

The Ministry of Health and Human Services will also need to monitor stock levels closely while the next orders remain pending, particularly for medicines that are essential to ongoing patient care.

For patients, the immediate improvement is welcome. But maintaining access to prescribed medicines will depend not only on current inventory, but also on the government’s ability to fund purchases and the availability of supplies from overseas.

Until the pending orders can proceed and future deliveries are secured, the hospital’s current recovery offers no guarantee against another shortage.

MELEKEOK, Palau (Oct. 30, 2026) — Ebil-Reklai Mitsko Walter, the head of Melekeok’s council of female chiefs and a leading traditional figure in Palau’s eastern region, died Wednesday, Oct. 21, after serving in the cultural role since March 2024.

Walter held the title of Ebil-Reklai, the female counterpart to the High Chief Reklai, and served as the head matriarch of Desbedall, Palau’s eastern region.

She assumed the title following the death of Ebil-Reklai Gracia Demei Yalap, who died July 1, 2023, after holding the title since 2009.

Walter’s death has prompted a traditional mourning period in Melekeok, as the community and the nation mourn her passing and her service to Palau.

By: Eoghan Olkeriil Ngirudelsang

NGCHESAR, Palau (Oct. 30, 2026) – The Ngchesar State Government has honored the late Chief Buk Desengang Emil Ramarui through Resolution No. 12-2, recognizing his lifelong service to Palau and to Ngchesar State.

The resolution highlights Ramarui’s many years of dedication to Palau’s education system and his wide-ranging service to the nation.

Ramarui was born on March 25, 1939, to Ramarui Ngchar and Ngitchob Erbai-Ramarui. He attended Ngchesar Elementary School, Emmaus High School, and the Pacific Territories Teacher School (PTTS) in Pohnpei. He later studied at the University of Guam from 1959 to 1962 and at the University of Hawaii’s East-West Center in 1970.

He began his career in education as a homeroom teacher at Emmaus High School. He later served as principal of Ngchesar Elementary School from 1962 to 1969.

From 1968 to 1970, Ramarui served as Teacher Supervisor for East Coast elementary schools under the Ministry of Education. He then became Assistant Supervisor of Public Elementary Schools from 1970 to 1981. In 1981, he was appointed Supervisor of Public Elementary Schools, a position he held until 1985.

From 1985 to 1999, Ramarui served as Chief of the Division of Public Elementary Education and Chief of the Division of School Management.

Beyond education, Ramarui served Palau in many public roles. He was a member of the ABC Board, War Claims Commission, PCAA Board, Palau Election Commission, Ngchesar State Flag Commission, and Palau Red Cross Society Board. He also served on the Palau Civil Service Pension Plan Board and became its chairman from 1996 to 1997.

Ramarui served in the House of Chiefs of the Sixth Palau Legislature from 1976 to 1977. He was also Chairman of the Ngchesar Development Authority and served on the Palau Community College Board of Trustees, the WCTC Board of Directors, the Palau Coral Reef Board, the Palau Code of Ethics Commission, and the Palau Parole Board. He became chairman of the Parole Board in 2005.

In traditional leadership, Ramarui was appointed by senior female members of Imengel clan to hold the fourth-ranking chief title, Buk, of the Ngara-Oldiang Council of Chiefs in Ngchesar beginning in 1984. On December 23, 1997, he was appointed by Ngermeketii clan and installed as Chief Desengang, the first chief of Ngeraus Hamlet in Ngchesar.

Following his passing, cultural observers note that the Ngara-Oldiang Council of Chiefs has lost three of its four top chiefs within a few months this year. The council has lost First Chief Ngirakebou, Second Chief Rechesengl, and Fourth Chief Buk. Many Palauans describe the loss of three senior chiefs in such a short period as rare and unprecedented in recent memory.

Late Chief Buk Desengang Emil Ramarui passed away on October 11, 2026. He was one of eight siblings. He and his late wife had six children, 14 grandchildren, and 11 great-grandchildren.

Ramarui will be laid to rest today Friday, October 30. His long life of service—as an educator, public servant, and traditional leader—leaves a lasting legacy for Ngchesar State and the Republic of Palau.

By Eoghan Olkeriil Ngirudelsang

KOROR, Palau (Oct. 30, 2026) — “The purpose of the bill is to modernize and streamline the election process,” Sen. Nakamura said as lawmakers considered a broad package of proposed election reforms that would change voting schedules, absentee-ballot procedures, voter registration and campaign rules.

The Senate Committee on Judiciary and Governmental Affairs held a public hearing Wednesday, Oct. 28, with the Palau Election Commission to discuss Senate Bill No. 12-11, which could expand beyond its original focus on absentee voting to address several aspects of Palau’s election system.

Nakamura said the bill passed its first reading on Feb. 4, 2025, and was assigned to the committee. Initially, the measure sought to establish earlier deadlines for sending absentee ballots and require that absentee votes be counted alongside ballots cast in Palau.

The committee is now considering additional provisions based on extensive feedback, including written comments from the election commission, Nakamura said.

The proposed changes aim to shorten the wait for absentee ballots, improve election administration and potentially allow complete election results to be announced once ballots cast in Palau and absentee votes have been counted.

Earlier deadlines and ballot printing

Nakamura said scheduling problems during the last national election highlighted the need to move key election deadlines earlier in the year.

“One of the issues I saw during the last national election was timing schedules. So, we will change all the deadlines for submission of nominating petitions because we have to push those so that they take place much earlier during the year,” he said.

He also cited a court case that significantly delayed ballot printing during the last election.

“If I am not mistaken, during the last election there was a court case which significantly delayed the printing of ballots,” Nakamura said.

The proposed changes would revise election timelines to address scheduling challenges.

Early voting, spending limits and voter registration

The committee is also considering early voting, limits on candidate spending during election cycles and measures to update the voter registration list.

Nakamura said campaign spending limits could help “even the playing field a little bit for candidates.”

Another proposal, introduced by Sen. Remengesau, would allow Palauan citizens to register to vote at age 16 so they would already be registered when they become eligible to vote at 18.

Nakamura said the proposal would be incorporated into Senate Bill No. 12-11, bringing the two measures together as a comprehensive election reform package.

Proposed anti-bribery rules

The bill would also establish specific prohibitions against bribing voters to influence their votes. Under the proposal, bribery would be classified as a Class C felony and could carry jail time.

The proposed definition would include giving voters money or items of monetary value.

Nakamura acknowledged that candidates in Palau commonly provide food and refreshments at meetings. He said the committee would consider these practices before determining what conduct should constitute bribery under Palauan election law.

The bill would also prohibit candidates and people working for them from providing notary services to voters living off-island.

Election commission supports early voting

Palau Election Commission Director Sherwin Yamaguchi said the commission has no objection to early voting and is capable of conducting it.

The commission already offers an option for registered voters who expect to be off-island on Election Day. Those voters can present their travel itineraries at the election office and cast their ballots ahead of time.

Yamaguchi pointed to Guam’s system as an example of additional options for voters.

“Currently while we’re having this discussion, early voting is being conducted in Guam in 2 ways where people can walk-in and vote or through drive-thru voting which caters to peoples with disabilities,” he said.

Some members of the Senate committee traveled to Guam to observe its election process and early-voting system.

Removing deceased voters from the rolls

Updating the voter registration list was another issue raised during the hearing.

The election commission said the list includes deceased individuals because it has no reliable way of learning when registered voters die.

Senators said the final version of the bill would include a mechanism for the hospital or the clerk of courts to provide death certificates or lists of deceased individuals to the commission so their names can be removed from the voter rolls.

Unifying election procedures remains a challenge

Palau has 16 states, each with its own constitution and election laws, making it difficult to align national and state elections on a single day.

If enacted, Senate Bill No. 12-11 would introduce changes affecting several parts of the election process, from candidate registration and campaign conduct to ballot handling and voter eligibility records.

Nakamura said the bill has passed its first reading, but substantial revisions remain before it returns to the Senate floor for second and third readings.

ANGAUR, Palau  (Oct. 24, 2026) –  “People, when they find artifacts such as World War II remnants or cultural artifacts, they usually take them, but now, with the museum, these items are brought to us,” said Angaur High School Principal Tiffany Kasiano.

An unused classroom at Angaur Elementary School is being transformed into a museum where residents can bring historical artifacts, students can learn about their island’s past, and visitors can discover Angaur’s distinctive cultural heritage.

The small museum already displays cases of World War II-era items, including an old short sword, glass bottles and ammunition parts. Other cases contain stone adzes dating to Angaur’s prehistoric period.

Angaur artifacts from WWII at the new Angaur Museum housed in the Angaur Elementary School. October 24, 2026. Island Times

Kasiano envisions expanding the collection and organizing the space to tell the story of Angaur, from its traditional and cultural history to its prehistoric beginnings, phosphate mining and role in World War II.

The project was made possible through Japan’s Grassroots Grant Program as part of the broader renovation and improvement of Angaur Elementary School.

The room formerly served as the school library. When the Ministry of Education moved the library into classrooms, the space became available for another purpose.

“When the Ministry of Education decided to bring the library into the classrooms, this became available and with support of Japan’s Grassroots Grant Program, we were able to renovate and change it into Angaur’s museum,” Kasiano said.

The planned museum will be divided into three sections. One will focus on Angaur’s traditional and cultural history, another on its prehistory, and the third on its modern history, including phosphate mining and World War II.

Kasiano said the school is working with an archaeologist and Palau High School students to help arrange the exhibits and organize the artifacts.

The team is also collecting and labeling items donated or brought in by residents, including artifacts found by people who were collecting bone remains and items recovered during construction under the TACMOR project.

For Kasiano, the museum is intended to do more than preserve objects from the past. It will serve as an educational resource for Angaur students while giving residents a place to safeguard and share pieces of their island’s history.

She also hopes to open the museum to tourists and other visitors to Angaur, allowing them to learn about the state’s history and cultural traditions.

Although Angaur is part of Palau, it has distinct cultural practices that differ from those of other parts of the country. According to Palauan legends, Angaur was the beginning of Palau and is also where Palauan souls go after death.

The island was also mined for phosphate by three different colonial powers and was heavily attacked during World War II, along with nearby Peleliu.

Through the classroom-turned-museum, Kasiano hopes to bring these layers of history together in one place, preserving Angaur’s past for the next generation while sharing it with those who come to learn about the island.
